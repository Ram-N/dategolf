# Marconi demonstrates wireless telegraphy — 1895

Memory aids:

### Look Back
Count forward from Hertz: Heinrich Hertz proved electromagnetic waves exist in 1887–88. Marconi turns that pure theory into a practical device about 7-8 years later, in 1895 — "it took less than a decade to go from 'waves exist' to 'let's send a message with them.'"

Sequential Tech Link: Heinirich Hertz proved electromagnetic waves existed in 1887. It took Marconi roughly 8 years to take Hertz's theoretical physics and turn it into a practical, working wireless telegraph system in 1895.

# Year of 
1895 was a remarkable year for new media:
In 1895, Marconi was demonstrating wireless communication while the Lumière brothers were introducing their motion-picture technology. So you can associate 1895 with the birth of two technologies that would transform mass culture: radio and cinema.

The Modern Tech Bookend: 1895 was a pivotal year for modern communication and media—it’s the exact same year the Lumière brothers gave the first commercial movie screening in Paris. Silent cinema and wireless radio were born together in 1895.

The "1-8-9-5" bridge trick: Marconi's breakthrough sits almost exactly midway between two other big 1890s science milestones — Röntgen discovers X-rays in 1895 too (same year!). Pairing "invisible waves" (X-rays) with "invisible signals" (radio waves) in the same year makes both stick.

# Look Ahead

Turn of the Century Anchor: Marconi’s breakthrough happened right at the tail end of the 19th century, exactly 5 years before 1900. Remembering it as a late-1890s Victorian invention helps prevent confusing it with the 1930s radio boom.

Marconi → Atlantic → 1901:
1895 is when Marconi demonstrated wireless telegraphy over land; just 6 years later, in 1901, he sent a wireless signal across the Atlantic. Think: 1895 = local breakthrough → 1901 = Atlantic breakthrough.

The "5 years before 1900" anchor:
Think of 1895 = just 5 years before the 20th century began. Wireless communication was therefore one of the technologies that arrived right at the threshold of the modern, mass-communication era.


Anchor to the Titanic: The Titanic disaster (1912) is famous partly because wireless distress signals saved survivors. Marconi's system was already 17 years old by then — so if you remember "wireless saved lives on the Titanic in 1912," subtract roughly two decades to land near 1895, when the technology was first demonstrated.
